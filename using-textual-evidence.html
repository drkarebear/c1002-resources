3. Support

Using Textual Evidence

Textual evidence is more than dropping a quotation into a paragraph. Choose the evidence that best supports your paragraph's claim, help the reader understand the moment, and explain what you see in it.

Keep in mind: Evidence does not do the analysis for you. It gives you something concrete to notice, interpret, and connect back to your argument.

Before You Practice: What Evidence Is For

In literary analysis, evidence gives your reader a reason to take your interpretation seriously. The goal is not to collect as many quotations as possible. The goal is to choose the detail that helps you make your particular point and then explain why it matters.

1. Evidence Is More Than Direct Quotation

You can support literary analysis in several ways. The best choice depends on what your paragraph is trying to show.

SummaryBriefly identify a relevant event or pattern when the overall action matters more than the exact wording.
ParaphraseRestate a relevant passage or moment in your own words when you need its meaning but not its exact phrasing.
Specific DetailPoint to a precise action, image, repetition, contrast, stage direction, structural choice, or other feature that gives you something concrete to analyze.
Direct QuotationUse the author's exact language when the wording itself matters: diction, imagery, tone, repetition, sound, phrasing, or a revealing line of dialogue.

Important: Summary and paraphrase are still evidence and still need citation when your assignment requires it. Follow your instructor's MLA directions for the text you are using.

2. Start with the Paragraph's Claim, Then Choose Evidence

A quotation can be beautiful, dramatic, or memorable and still be the wrong evidence for the paragraph you are writing. Ask: What is this paragraph trying to prove?

Paragraph claim: Mara's attempts to sound calm reveal how strongly she wants to avoid confronting a warning.
Evidence that fits: Mara says, “It's fine,” while folding the warning letter into smaller and smaller squares.
Evidence that may belong somewhere else: The room has blue curtains and two windows.

The first detail creates a useful contradiction between what Mara says and what Mara does. That gives the writer something to analyze.

3. Use the Amount of Evidence You Actually Need

More quotation does not automatically mean more support. A short phrase can do more analytical work than a full paragraph if that phrase contains the word, image, or action your interpretation depends on.

  • If the exact wording matters, quote the useful word or phrase.
  • If the event or idea matters more than the wording, summarize or paraphrase it.
  • If one small detail reveals a pattern, focus on that detail.
  • Avoid making the reader search through a long quotation for the part you mean.

4. What Should Happen Around the Evidence?

A useful analytical sequence is:

ContextWhere are we? Who is speaking or acting? What does the reader need to know?
EvidenceGive the focused quotation, paraphrase, summary, or detail that supports the paragraph.
NoticePoint to a word, image, action, contrast, pattern, or structural choice in the evidence.
ExplainWhat does that choice suggest, reveal, complicate, or help us understand?
ConnectHow does this analysis help prove the paragraph's claim or the essay's larger thesis?

These are jobs your writing needs to accomplish, not a required five-sentence formula. You may combine moves, repeat evidence and analysis, or spend several sentences unpacking one detail.

5. Three Common Ways to Introduce a Short Quotation

Embed the phrase

The doorway feels “enormous” from the child's perspective.

No comma is needed just because the quotation begins.

Speaking signal phrase

Mara admits, “I never opened it.”

A speaking phrase commonly takes a comma before a full quoted sentence.

Complete sentence + colon

The poem turns at the moment of recognition: “The house remembers what I do not.”

A colon can introduce a quotation after a complete sentence.

Quotation punctuation depends on the grammar of your sentence. The goal is to make the evidence part of your own writing rather than dropping it between sentences.

6. Watch One Piece of Evidence Become Analysis

Practice passage: “It's fine,” Mara said, folding the warning letter into smaller and smaller squares before sliding it beneath her cup.

Paragraph claim

Mara's attempts to sound calm reveal how strongly she wants to avoid confronting the warning.

Context + evidence

After reading the warning, Mara tries to dismiss its importance. She says, “It's fine,” while folding the letter into “smaller and smaller squares.”

Notice + explain

The repeated word “smaller” turns her nervous movement into an attempt to contain the problem physically. Her actions contradict the calmness of her words.

Connect

That contradiction supports the paragraph's claim that Mara is not relaxed at all; she is trying to make the warning feel easier to avoid.

Notice what did most of the work: not the quotation by itself, but the writer's explanation of the repeated word, the physical action, and the contradiction between speech and behavior.

Try It With Your Text

Choose one paragraph claim or topic sentence from something you are currently writing. Then ask:

  • What kind of evidence fits this claim best? Summary, paraphrase, a specific detail, or direct quotation?
  • What is the smallest useful amount?
  • What exactly do I want my reader to notice in it?
  • What does that detail suggest or reveal?
  • How does my explanation connect back to this paragraph's claim?
