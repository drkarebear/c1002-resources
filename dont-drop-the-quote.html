Don't Drop the Quote

Textual evidence works best when your reader understands where it comes from, why you chose it, and what you want them to notice about it.

Before You Practice: Evidence Is Not the Analysis

This lab begins after you have already chosen relevant evidence. If you need help deciding what to quote, paraphrase, or summarize, use Using Textual Evidence. Here, the question is: What do I say after the evidence?

EVIDENCEGive the reader the relevant textual moment.
↓
ZOOM INPoint to a word, image, action, contrast, repetition, silence, stage direction, or other specific feature.
↓
EXPLAINWhat does that feature suggest, reveal, complicate, or help us understand?
↓
CONNECTHow does the explanation help support the paragraph's claim?
Do not make the reader do the interpretive work for you. The reader can see the quotation. Your job is to show what you see in it.

Restating Is Not the Same as Analyzing

Evidence

“She clutched the letter.”

Restatement

This shows that she holds the letter tightly.

Analysis

The verb “clutched” makes the action feel tense rather than casual, suggesting that the letter carries emotional weight she is not ready to release.

The analytical version does not simply translate the quotation. It notices a choice in the language and makes an inference about its significance.

Sometimes One Word Is Enough

You do not always need to explain an entire quotation at once. Often the strongest analysis slows down around the smallest useful detail.

Quotation: “I swept the room clean / but your name stayed in the dust.”

Zoom in: Why does the speaker use “but”?

Explain: The word turns the second line against the speaker's attempt to clean the room, making memory feel resistant to deliberate removal.

Connect: The contrast supports the paragraph's claim that the speaker cannot simply decide to leave the memory behind.

Do Not Rush to the Next Quotation

Writers sometimes add quotation after quotation because the paragraph feels too short. But a paragraph may need more explanation, not more evidence.

  • What exact word, action, image, or contrast should my reader notice?
  • Why might the author have made that choice?
  • What does the detail suggest beyond its literal meaning?
  • How does my explanation support or complicate the paragraph's claim?
  • Have I stayed with the evidence long enough before moving on?
“This quote shows...” is not automatically wrong. The problem is what comes next. If the sentence only repeats the quotation, analysis has not happened yet.

Round 1: What Comes After the Evidence?

PARAGRAPH IDEA

EVIDENCE TO USE

You practiced turning evidence into analysis.

Before you add a quotation, ask:

  • Does my reader need a little context?
  • Can I integrate the evidence into my own sentence?
  • Have I returned to my own voice afterward?
  • Am I explaining the important language instead of repeating it?

A quotation gives you evidence. Your analysis tells the reader why that evidence matters.
