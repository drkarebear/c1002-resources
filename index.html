Where Are You Stuck?

You do not need to know whether your problem is “analysis,” “interpretation,” or “development.” Choose the sentence that sounds most like what is happening for you.
Not sure which one fits? Start with What Is Literary Analysis?. You can come back here whenever you need a different kind of help.

“I read it, but I don’t understand it.” Try Lost in the Text? to find the exact place the reading became confusing. “I understand what happened, but I don’t know what counts as analysis.” Start with What Kind of Thinking Is This? “I noticed something, but I don’t know what it means.” Go to Interpret and practice turning observations into possible meanings. “I have an idea, but I don’t know how to prove it.” Go to Support for evidence, quotations, and explanation. “I have a draft, but I don’t know what to fix.” Use Literary Analysis Triage to find the highest-impact revision. “My professor wrote a comment I don’t understand.” Use What Does My Professor Mean? to turn feedback into a next move. “I don’t know what this literary term means.” Use Literary Terms Made Simple for plain-language definitions and examples. “I’m supposed to close read, but I don’t know what that means.” Use Close Reading Made Visible to see the process in a story, poem, and play. “I’m not sure how to read a story, poem, or play.” Use How to Read Literature for simple genre-specific reading strategies.
You are not expected to know all of this already. College literature has its own vocabulary and habits of thinking. These pages are here so you can learn them as you go.

Choose a Move

If you know where you are getting stuck, go directly to that move. If you are not sure, begin with Notice.

1

Notice

See what the text is doing.

  • Tell summary from analysis
  • Notice details and patterns
  • Ask useful questions
  • Zoom in on language
Practice Notice
2

Interpret

Ask what the pattern might mean.

  • Move from observation to interpretation
  • Test whether a reading fits the evidence
  • Build thematic ideas
  • Analyze perspective
Practice Interpret
3

Support

Use evidence and explain it.

  • Connect claims to evidence
  • Integrate quotations
  • Explain how evidence supports an idea
Practice Support
4

Develop

Build and complicate an argument.

  • Strengthen a thesis
  • Revise when evidence complicates a claim
  • Repair weak analysis
Practice Develop

How to Use the Labs

  • Choose the move that matches where you feel stuck.
  • Read the feedback, even when your first answer is correct.
  • Try again when something does not work.
  • You do not need to complete every activity unless your instructor asks you to.
  • If your instructor requires proof, use the completion screen at the end of the activity.

More visibility.

Literary readers do many things that are easy to leave invisible: making sense of a difficult passage, noticing a pattern, deciding what might matter, moving from evidence to interpretation, explaining a quotation, and revising an idea when the text gets more complicated. These labs make those processes easier to see, practice, and use.

Small tools. Real literary analysis.

The collection is designed to support students without lowering the intellectual work of ENGL C1002.

Make the hidden move visible.

Break literary reading and analysis into decisions students can see, practice, and transfer to their own texts.

Start from the problem.

Help students find a tool by what they are experiencing, not by whether they already know literary or academic terminology.

Keep agency with the learner.

Offer strategies, retries, choices, and opportunities to test an interpretation rather than prescribing one “right” reading.
