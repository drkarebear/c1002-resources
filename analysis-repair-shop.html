The Analysis Repair Shop

Weak literary analysis is usually not “bad writing” in some mysterious way. It often has a specific, repairable problem: too much summary, a dropped quotation, an unsupported claim, or evidence that never gets explained.

Before You Practice: Diagnose Before You Rewrite

The Analysis Repair Shop works at the paragraph level. If you are not sure which part of an entire draft deserves attention first, start with Literary Analysis Triage. Once you have a paragraph that needs work, this lab helps you identify its biggest analytical gap and choose a repair.

1. READ FOR THE PARAGRAPH'S JOBWhat is this paragraph trying to prove?

2. DIAGNOSE THE BIGGEST GAPIs the problem the claim, evidence, explanation, connection, or complexity?

4. READ AGAINAfter the repair, decide whether another problem still needs attention.
Do not try to fix everything at once. A paragraph may have several imperfections, but one analytical problem usually matters more than sentence polish, transition words, or adding another quotation.

Five Common Analytical Repairs

CLAIM REPAIR The paragraph summarizes a topic or event but never makes an interpretive point.
EVIDENCE REPAIR The paragraph makes a promising claim but gives little or no textual support.
EXPLANATION REPAIR Evidence appears, but the writer restates it instead of explaining what it suggests.
CONNECTION REPAIR The paragraph analyzes a detail but never shows how that analysis supports the paragraph's larger claim.
COMPLEXITY REPAIR The claim ignores an exception, contradiction, ambiguity, or other evidence that makes the interpretation less simple.

Fix the Reasoning Before the Polish

Paragraph: The narrator mentions the photograph three times. “She turns it face down.” This quote shows that she turns the photograph over. Furthermore, this is symbolism.

You could edit: replace “Furthermore,” vary the vocabulary, or fix sentence rhythm.

But the bigger repair is: explain why turning the photograph face down matters.

Possible repair: Turning the photograph face down does not remove it from the room; it only blocks the image from view. The gesture therefore makes avoidance feel temporary and deliberate, supporting the paragraph's claim that the narrator is trying to control when she confronts the memory.

Once the reasoning works, sentence-level editing becomes worthwhile. Until then, polishing may only make a weak analytical move sound smoother.

Use One Diagnostic Question at a Time

  • Claim: What is this paragraph trying to prove?
  • Evidence: What specific part of the text gives me something to examine?
  • Explanation: What should the reader notice in that evidence, and what does it suggest?
  • Connection: How does that explanation help support the paragraph's claim?
  • Complexity: Is there evidence that requires the claim to become more precise?
A diagnosis should point toward an action. “Needs work” is not very useful. “The evidence is here, but I only paraphrase it” tells you exactly what to revise next.

Round 1: Diagnose the Biggest Gap

PARAGRAPH UNDER REPAIR

You practiced diagnosing and repairing literary analysis.

When a paragraph feels weak, try diagnosing it with one question at a time:

  • Am I summarizing instead of interpreting?
  • Is my claim specific and supportable?
  • Did I give evidence?
  • Did I explain the evidence instead of repeating it?
  • Did I name a literary device without explaining what it does?
  • Did I make a claim that goes farther than the evidence?

Revision gets easier when you can name the problem. A paragraph is not broken all at once; it usually needs one repair at a time.

Try It With Your Draft

Choose one paragraph from your own draft. Name its biggest problem in a few words, such as too much summary, needs evidence, or evidence needs explanation. Fix that one problem before trying to repair everything else.
