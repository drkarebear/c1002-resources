4. Develop Practice

Titles, Introductions & Conclusions

Practice writing the parts that frame a literary-analysis essay: a useful title, an introduction that gets to the interpretive problem, and a conclusion that shows why the argument matters.

Keep in mind: These parts are not formulas. Their job is to help a reader enter, follow, and leave your interpretation.

Before You Practice: Frame the Argument

The title, introduction, and conclusion should help the reader understand the argument you are building. They do not need to sound dramatic or overly formal. Their job is to make the essay’s direction clear.

1. Create a Title That Gives the Reader a Clue

A literary-analysis title is usually more engaging when it hints at the specific idea you are exploring, not just the name of the assignment.

Literary analysis titles love a good colon. A colon lets you pair something memorable with something specific: interesting phrase or big idea: analytical focus and text. The first half catches the reader’s attention; the second half tells the reader what the essay is actually analyzing.

Too generic

An Analysis of The Emperor’s New Clothes

The reader knows the text, but not what your essay is actually about.

More focused

The Pressure to Agree: Public Performance in The Emperor’s New Clothes

The reader can already see the essay’s interpretive direction.

A very useful pattern:
Interesting phrase or idea: Specific analytical focus

The Pressure to Agree: Public Performance in The Emperor’s New Clothes
Mirrors and Measurement: Comparison, Beauty, and Power in Snow White
“Keeps Her Safe”: Protection and Confinement in Rapunzel

Useful title patterns:

  • Key idea + text: Comparison and Control in Snow White
  • Interesting phrase + explanation: “Keeps Her Safe”: Protection and Confinement in Rapunzel
  • Tension + text: Wanting Freedom, Fearing Change in The Red Shoes
  • Short creative phrase + analytical subtitle: The Pressure to Agree: Public Performance in The Emperor’s New Clothes
Try this: Write down the most interesting idea in your thesis. Then ask, “Can my title give the reader a hint about this idea?”

Formatting note: Your essay title itself is not italicized. Inside the title, format the literary work the way you normally would: book and play titles are usually italicized, while short-story and poem titles are usually placed in quotation marks.

2. Build the Introduction with HATMAT

A useful planning checklist for a literary-analysis introduction is HATMAT. You do not need one sentence for every letter, and you do not have to follow the checklist mechanically. Use it to make sure the reader gets the information needed to understand your argument.

HHook

Open with an idea, tension, or question that connects directly to your argument. Avoid a giant statement like “Since the beginning of time…”

AAuthor

Name the author when you introduce the literary work.

TTitle

Identify the work you are analyzing and format its title appropriately for the genre.

MMain Character(s) or Central Situation

Orient the reader to the character, speaker, relationship, or conflict your argument actually needs.

AA Short Summary

Give only enough context to understand the analytical problem. Do not retell the entire plot.

TThesis

End the introduction by stating the specific, debatable interpretation your essay will develop.

Think of HATMAT as a checklist, not a formula. A poem may not have a “main character,” for example, so you might identify the speaker or central situation instead.

Mini example: Social pressure can make people distrust what they see with their own eyes. In Hans Christian Andersen’s The Emperor’s New Clothes, officials and townspeople publicly praise clothing they cannot actually see because they fear appearing foolish. The child’s eventual interruption exposes how fragile that agreement is. By showing adult after adult repeat a claim they privately doubt, Andersen suggests that fear of social judgment can make conformity stronger than individual perception.

3. End with Significance, Not Just Repetition

A conclusion should give the reader a sense of closure while showing what your analysis has helped us understand. You can return to the thesis, but do not simply copy and paste it with a few synonyms.

  • Return to your central claim in fresh language.
  • Pull the evidence together. What becomes clearer when we consider the patterns as a whole?
  • Answer the final “so what?” What larger idea, tension, or human problem does your interpretation help us see?
  • Do not introduce a brand-new argument that would require another body paragraph to prove.
  • Keep it focused. One developed paragraph is often enough.
Instead of: “In conclusion, the crowd agrees because they are afraid.”
Try: “The child’s interruption matters because it reveals how fragile the crowd’s agreement actually is. Once one person says what everyone can see, the performance becomes harder to maintain, suggesting that social pressure depends partly on the belief that no one else will break from the group.”

Quick Recap

Title: signal the text and interpretive focus. Introduction: give only the context the argument needs, identify the problem or pattern, and arrive at a working thesis. Conclusion: return to the central tension and explain what the analysis has made clearer, more complex, or worth noticing.
