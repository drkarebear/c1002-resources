How to Read Literature

Stories, poems, and plays do not always ask you to read in exactly the same way. This page gives you a few simple things to look for so you do not have to wonder, “What am I supposed to notice?”

As you read, these five questions can help turn ordinary reading notes into material you can later use for analysis.

You do not need to understand everything on the first read. Your first job is to understand enough to keep going. Then you can return, notice something specific, and start asking what it might mean.

Before You Start

  • Read once for basic understanding. You do not need to analyze every sentence while you are still figuring out what is happening.
  • Mark confusion, not everything. If a line, image, or moment feels strange, put a question mark next to it and keep going if you can.
  • Notice what repeats or changes. Repetition and shifts often give you somewhere useful to look again.
  • Do not hunt for a hidden answer. Literary analysis is about building a reasonable interpretation from evidence.

Reading a Story

Short stories · novels · narrative fiction

Start with what changes

In a story, it is easy to focus only on plot. Plot matters, but analysis often begins when you notice how a character, relationship, setting, pattern, or point of view changes or creates tension.

What to notice
  • What a character wants
  • What gets in the way
  • What changes
  • Repeated objects, images, or phrases
  • Contrasts between what characters say and do
  • Who tells the story and what they know
  • Places that feel safe, threatening, open, or closed
Questions that can lead to analysis
  • Why does this character react this way?
  • Why does this detail keep appearing?
  • What does the narrator notice or avoid?
  • What changes between the beginning and the end?
  • What does the story make easy or difficult for this character?
Example:

If a character keeps standing near a doorway whenever they must make a decision, do not stop at “the door is a symbol.” Notice the pattern first. Then ask: Why does the story keep connecting doors with moments of choice?

1Notice

The doorway keeps appearing.

2Interpret

Maybe it connects physical movement with difficult choices.

3Support

Find another scene where the pattern appears.

4Develop

Ask whether every doorway means the same thing.

Reading a Poem

Poetry

Slow down and read more than once

Poems often pack a lot into a small space. You may need to read once for the basic situation, then read again for words, images, sounds, line breaks, patterns, and shifts.

What to notice
  • Who seems to be speaking
  • Who the speaker may be talking to
  • Words or images that repeat
  • Comparisons and figurative language
  • Line breaks that create surprise or emphasis
  • Changes in tone or mood
  • Where the poem turns or shifts
Helpful reading moves
  • Read the poem once without stopping.
  • Read it again aloud if you can.
  • Read across line breaks to recover the sentence.
  • Circle one repeated or surprising word.
  • Mark the place where the poem seems to change direction.
Example:

If a poem says, “The empty swing / keeps moving / though the yard is still,” first make sure you understand the sentence across the line breaks. Then ask why the moving swing is placed against a still yard.

1Notice

The swing moves while everything else is still.

2Interpret

The image may connect absence with something that continues.

3Support

Look for other images of movement or stillness.

4Develop

Ask whether the poem treats memory as comforting, unsettling, or both.

Reading a Play

Drama

Read the actions, not just the dialogue

In a play, meaning comes from more than what characters say. Pay attention to stage directions, silence, entrances, exits, objects, interruptions, and what characters avoid saying.

What to notice
  • Who is present in the scene
  • Who enters or leaves
  • What characters do while speaking
  • Pauses or silence
  • Repeated objects or gestures
  • What characters know that others do not
  • Differences between words and actions
Questions that can lead to analysis
  • What does the action show that the dialogue does not?
  • Why does this character avoid answering directly?
  • What changes when someone enters or leaves?
  • What does the audience know that a character does not?
  • Why might this pause or silence matter?
Example:

A stage direction says: “Mara folds the unopened note twice and places it beneath her cup before Eli enters.” The note is unopened, but Mara already hides it. That action may matter more than anything she says next.

1Notice

Mara hides an unopened note.

2Interpret

She may already fear what the note represents.

3Support

Use the stage direction as evidence.

4Develop

Compare the action with what Mara later says to Eli.

If You Get Stuck

Try the problem that sounds most like yours:
  • “I do not understand the passage.” Try Lost in the Text?
  • “I know what happened, but I do not know what to notice.” Start with Notice.
  • “I noticed something, but I do not know what it means.” Go to Interpret.
  • “I have an idea, but I do not know how to prove it.” Go to Support.
  • “I have a draft, but I do not know what to fix.” Try Literary Analysis Triage.
  • “I do not know what a literary term means.” Use Literary Terms Made Simple.

One Last Thing

You are not supposed to notice everything.

Strong literary reading often starts with one small thing that feels interesting, strange, repeated, confusing, or important. You can look again, test an idea, change your mind, and build from there.
