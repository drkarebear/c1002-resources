Literary Terms Made Simple

You do not need to memorize every literary term before you can analyze a text. Use this page when you run into a word you do not know, or when you want help figuring out what to notice.

The term is not the analysis. Knowing that something is a metaphor, symbol, or example of imagery is only the beginning. The useful question is usually: What does this choice do, and why might it matter?

You can search by the literary term or by a plain-language word like “speaker,” “comparison,” or “repetition.”

Stories and Narrators

These terms help you think about who is telling a story, what characters do, and how the story is organized.

Story term

Narrator

Plain meaning: The voice that tells a story.

Look for:

What the narrator notices, knows, misunderstands, hides, or leaves out.

Ask:

How does this narrator shape what I believe or notice?

Example:

A narrator insists, “I was not afraid,” but keeps describing every sound in the hallway. That tension may make the narrator seem less reliable or more frightened than they admit.

Story term

Point of View

Plain meaning: The position from which we experience the story.

Look for:

Who can see what, whose thoughts we know, and whose experience is missing.

Ask:

What does this perspective allow me to know, and what does it keep uncertain?

Story term

Characterization

Plain meaning: The way a writer builds our understanding of a character.

Look for:

What a character says, does, avoids, notices, remembers, or how others respond to them.

Ask:

What do these choices suggest about the character that the text does not state directly?

Story term

Setting

Plain meaning: Where and when a text takes place.

Look for:

Places that feel safe, threatening, open, closed, public, private, familiar, or strange.

Ask:

How does this place or time shape what the characters can do or how the scene feels?

Story term

Conflict

Plain meaning: A struggle, pressure, or tension in a text.

Look for:

What a character wants, what gets in the way, and what competing values or desires appear.

Ask:

What larger idea becomes visible through this struggle?

Story term

Structure

Plain meaning: The way a text is arranged.

Look for:

What comes first, what is delayed, what repeats, where the text shifts, and how it ends.

Ask:

Why might the writer have arranged the text this way?

Story term

Flashback

Plain meaning: A moment when the text moves back to an earlier event.

Look for:

A memory, earlier scene, or shift away from the present timeline.

Ask:

Why does the text reveal this earlier event here instead of earlier?

Language and Images

These terms help you slow down and notice what particular words or images are doing.

Language term

Diction

Plain meaning: The particular words a writer chooses.

Look for:

A word that feels unusually strong, formal, casual, harsh, gentle, repeated, or surprising.

Ask:

Why this word instead of a simpler or more expected one?

Example:

“The hallway swallowed his footsteps.” The word “swallowed” makes the hallway feel active and possibly threatening, not merely quiet.

Language term

Imagery

Plain meaning: Language that helps you imagine a sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, or other sensory experience.

Look for:

Details that make you see, hear, feel, smell, or otherwise experience the scene.

Ask:

What does this image make especially vivid, and why might that matter?

Language term

Figurative Language

Plain meaning: Language that is not meant only in a literal way.

Look for:

Comparisons, exaggeration, personification, or images that ask you to imagine one thing through another.

Ask:

What becomes easier, stranger, stronger, or more emotional because of this nonliteral language?

Language term

Metaphor

Plain meaning: A comparison that treats one thing as if it were another.

Look for:

A description that gives one thing the qualities of something else.

Ask:

What qualities from the comparison get transferred, and what do they add?

Language term

Simile

Plain meaning: A comparison that usually uses “like” or “as.”

Look for:

Two unlike things being compared directly.

Ask:

What does the comparison help me see differently?

Language term

Personification

Plain meaning: Giving human actions or qualities to something nonhuman.

Look for:

Objects, weather, places, or ideas that seem to act, feel, want, watch, or speak.

Ask:

How does making this thing seem human change the mood or meaning?

Language term

Symbol / Symbolism

Plain meaning: A concrete thing that takes on a larger meaning in the text.

Look for:

An object, place, color, image, or action that repeats or becomes unusually important.

Ask:

What larger idea might this connect to, and what evidence makes that reading plausible?

Important:

A symbol does not have one automatic meaning. A bird does not always mean freedom. The text has to help build that meaning.

Patterns and Meaning

These terms help you connect details and think about larger ideas.

Pattern term

Repetition

Plain meaning: A word, image, phrase, sound, action, or idea that appears more than once.

Look for:

Something that keeps returning.

Ask:

What changes when this appears again? What pattern is being built?

Pattern term

Contrast

Plain meaning: A noticeable difference between two things placed near each other.

Look for:

Light/dark, silence/noise, inside/outside, what a character says versus what they do, or two very different characters or scenes.

Ask:

What becomes clearer because these two things are different?

Pattern term

Motif

Plain meaning: A recurring image, idea, object, phrase, or situation that helps build meaning.

Look for:

A repeated detail that seems connected to a larger idea.

Ask:

What larger pattern does this repeated detail help create?

Meaning term

Theme

Plain meaning: An idea the text develops about a larger topic.

Look for:

Patterns involving topics like family, power, freedom, identity, fear, belonging, memory, or knowledge.

Ask:

What does this particular text seem to suggest about that topic?

Not quite:

“The theme is family.”

Stronger:

“The story suggests that family loyalty can become a form of pressure when belonging depends on silence.”

Meaning term

Tone

Plain meaning: The attitude or emotional quality created by the speaker's or narrator's language.

Look for:

Word choice, sentence rhythm, exaggeration, understatement, repetition, or changes in how something is described.

Ask:

How does the language make the speaker or narrator seem to feel about the subject?

Meaning term

Irony

Plain meaning: A meaningful gap between what is expected, said, known, or intended and what is actually true or happens.

Look for:

A difference between words and meaning, expectation and outcome, or what one character knows versus another.

Ask:

What does this gap make the reader notice or understand?

Pattern term

Foreshadowing

Plain meaning: A detail that hints at something that may become important later.

Look for:

An early image, warning, object, line, or event that gains new meaning later.

Ask:

How does the earlier detail change once I know what happens later?

Poetry

Poetry often asks you to pay attention to the voice, the line, and the way language is arranged on the page.

Poetry term

Speaker

Plain meaning: The voice speaking in a poem.

Important:

The speaker is not automatically the poet.

Ask:

Who seems to be speaking, to whom, and what does this voice reveal or hide?

Poetry term

Stanza

Plain meaning: A group of lines in a poem, somewhat like a paragraph.

Look for:

Where one stanza ends and another begins, and whether the poem shifts in subject, tone, image, or idea there.

Ask:

Why might the poem divide its ideas at this point?

Poetry term

Line Break

Plain meaning: The place where one line of a poem ends and the next begins.

Look for:

A break that creates a pause, surprise, double meaning, emphasis, or unusual connection.

Ask:

What changes because the line ends here?

Poetry term

Rhyme

Plain meaning: Repeated or similar sounds, often at the ends of words.

Look for:

Words that echo each other and places where a rhyme pattern changes or breaks.

Ask:

What ideas or words get connected because they sound alike?

Poetry term

Rhythm

Plain meaning: The pattern and pace of stressed and unstressed sounds in language.

Look for:

Language that speeds up, slows down, becomes regular, or suddenly breaks its pattern.

Ask:

How does the pace or beat affect the feeling of the poem?

Drama

Plays communicate through spoken words, actions, silence, and stage directions.

Drama term

Dialogue

Plain meaning: The words characters speak to one another.

Look for:

What characters say, avoid saying, repeat, interrupt, misunderstand, or imply.

Ask:

What does this conversation reveal beyond the literal words?

Drama term

Stage Direction

Plain meaning: Written instructions in a play that describe action, movement, setting, sound, or how something happens.

Look for:

Actions that do not match what a character says, meaningful pauses, entrances, exits, or objects being handled.

Ask:

What does the action show that the dialogue does not say?

Drama term

Scene

Plain meaning: A section of a play, often organized around a particular place, time, or group of characters.

Look for:

Who enters, who leaves, what changes, and what the scene accomplishes before it ends.

Ask:

What changes because this scene happened?

Drama term

Dramatic Irony

Plain meaning: When the audience knows something important that a character does not.

Look for:

A character making decisions without information the audience already has.

Ask:

How does knowing more than the character change the tension or meaning of the scene?

Words You May Hear in Literary Analysis

These are common college words instructors may use when they talk about reading and writing.

Analysis word

Analysis

Plain meaning: Explaining how or why something in the text matters.

Shortcut:

Notice something → ask why it matters → explain your idea.

Analysis word

Interpretation

Plain meaning: A possible explanation of what a text or pattern might mean.

Important:

More than one interpretation can be reasonable if it fits the evidence.

Writing word

Claim

Plain meaning: An idea you are asking your reader to consider or believe.

Ask:

What am I actually saying about this text?

Writing word

Textual Evidence

Plain meaning: The words, actions, images, patterns, stage directions, or other details from the text that help support your idea.

Important:

Evidence is not only a long quotation. A single word or small pattern can be powerful evidence.

Analysis word

Close Reading

Plain meaning: Slowing down and looking carefully at a small part of the text.

Look for:

Specific words, images, repetitions, contrasts, shifts, sounds, actions, or structural choices.

Writing word

Thesis

Plain meaning: The main interpretive claim your essay develops.

Important:

Your thesis can change as you read, write, and discover more. That is normal.

Analysis word

Complicate

Plain meaning: Make an idea more accurate by including tension, exceptions, or evidence that does not fit perfectly.

Example:

Instead of “The character wants freedom,” you might discover: “The character wants freedom, yet repeatedly chooses the safety of familiar restrictions.”

What Should You Do With These Terms?

You do not need to use literary vocabulary just to sound academic. Use a term when it helps you notice something more clearly or explain your interpretation more precisely.
