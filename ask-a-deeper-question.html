4. Develop

Ask a Deeper Question

A deeper question does not have to sound more academic or make a bigger claim. It helps you turn a broad idea into a more specific interpretive problem that the text can actually help you explore.

Before You Practice: Make the Question More Useful, Not Just Bigger

Broad ideas such as power, identity, freedom, belonging, or family can help you notice what a text is exploring. The next step is to ask a question that gives you something more precise to investigate.

1. Move From Topic to Interpretive Problem

BROAD IDEAThere is a power dynamic in this relationship.
↓
BETTER QUESTIONWho has power, and how do we know?
↓
DEEPER QUESTIONHow is that power maintained or challenged, and what does the text suggest about why the relationship continues?

The deeper question is not “better” because it is longer. It is better because it asks about how the pattern works and why it matters.

2. Useful Questions Often Ask About Relationships, Tensions, or Consequences

HOW?How is this pattern created, maintained, changed, or resisted?
WHO?Who benefits, who is limited, who gets believed, or who pays the cost?
WHAT TENSION?What two needs, values, identities, or possibilities seem to conflict?
WHAT CHANGES?When does the pattern shift, and what causes that change?
WHAT DOES IT COST?What does a character gain, lose, hide, or give up?
WHAT MIGHT IT SUGGEST?What larger interpretation begins to emerge from the pattern?

3. “Deeper” Does Not Mean More Sweeping

Too broad

What does this story say about human nature?

More useful

Why does the character keep seeking the group's approval even after recognizing that belonging requires her to hide part of herself?

The second question stays closer to the text. It creates room for interpretation without requiring the essay to explain all people or all of society.

4. The Best Question Is One the Text Gives You Evidence to Explore

Before using a question to develop your thesis, check:

  • Does it grow from something I actually noticed in the text?
  • Could more than one reasonable answer be possible?
  • Can I point to several moments or details that would help me investigate it?
  • Does it move beyond labeling something “good,” “bad,” “fair,” or “unfair”?
  • Would answering it make my interpretation more specific, complicated, or arguable?
You do not need the answer before you ask the question. A useful analytical question helps you reread with a purpose. Your answer can develop as you examine more evidence.

Try It With Your Text

Choose one broad idea you have already noticed. Then write a question that asks about how it works, who it affects, what tension it creates, what it costs, or what the text may be suggesting about it. Make sure the question grows from evidence you can actually return to.

Try this sentence frame: “I noticed ________. A deeper question I could ask is ________.”
