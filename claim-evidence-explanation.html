Literary Analysis Lab

Claim, Evidence, or Explanation?

Strong literary-analysis paragraphs usually do more than make a point and add a quotation. They help the reader see what you are arguing, what in the text supports it, and how the evidence connects to the idea.

What This Helps You Do

You will practice identifying the three jobs that sentences often perform in literary analysis.

CLAIM Makes an interpretive point that the paragraph will develop.
EVIDENCE Gives a specific detail, quotation, or example from the text.
EXPLANATION Shows how the evidence supports the claim and why the detail matters.
A quotation is not the explanation. Evidence gives your reader something to examine. Your explanation does the interpretive work.

You practiced the architecture of an analytical paragraph.

A useful pattern to remember is:

CLAIM: What am I arguing?
EVIDENCE: What in the text gives me a reason to argue this?
EXPLANATION: How does that evidence support my interpretation?

Real paragraphs can be more complex than this, and explanation may take several sentences. But if a paragraph has a quotation and no explanation, that is usually where the analytical gap is.
