Instructor Resources

For Instructors

Created by Karen Crozer for ENGL C1002 classes. These resources break literary analysis into smaller, low-stakes moves that can be assigned individually, embedded in Canvas, or offered as targeted help when a student gets stuck.

No student data is collected by these labs. Activity responses stay in the student's browser. The Reflect button gives students an activity-specific prompt to answer in Canvas; the site itself does not receive the response.

Recommended by Assignment Stage

If you know where students are in an assignment, this is the fastest place to start.

Before or During Reading

Help students enter the text

Try: How to Read Literature, Literary Terms Made Simple, Lost in the Text?, Notice → Wonder, or Pattern Finder.

Best when: students are confused by genre, vocabulary, difficult passages, or what they are supposed to notice.

After Reading / Before Drafting

Move from notes to an idea

Try: So What?, Build an Interpretation, Theme or Topic?, Whose Eyes Are We Seeing Through?, or Strengthen the Thesis.

Best when: students understand the text but are still asking, “What could I actually argue?”

While Drafting

Build analytical paragraphs

Try: Claim, Evidence, or Explanation?, Don't Drop the Quote, Zoom In, and Strengthen the Thesis.

Best when: students have ideas and evidence but need help turning them into analytical writing.

During Revision

Decide what to improve first

Try: Literary Analysis Triage, The Analysis Repair Shop, Complicate It, and Don't Drop the Quote.

Best when: students have a substantial draft and need a high-impact revision plan.

After Instructor Feedback

Turn comments into actions

Try: What Does My Professor Mean? followed by the lab that matches the feedback.

Best when: comments such as “go deeper,” “too much summary,” or “explain the quote” are not yet translating into revision.

Optional Review

Terminology without a vocabulary test

Try: Practice Literary Terms.

Best when: students want extra practice recognizing common terms. It is optional, low-stakes, and unscored.

Simple Ways to Use the Site

1. Let Students Choose Based on Need

Best when students are at different points or you want to reduce busywork.

Canvas-ready direction:
Open the Literary Analysis Labs student home. Under “Where Are You Stuck?” choose the statement that best matches what you need right now. Complete one recommended activity. At the end, use Reflect and respond to the prompt in Canvas.

2. Respond to Draft Feedback

Best after you return a paragraph or essay.

Canvas-ready direction:
Review the feedback on your draft. Use “What Does My Professor Mean?” if you are unsure what a comment is asking you to do. Complete one lab that addresses the most important revision need, then apply that move to your own draft.

3. Target One Course Skill

Best as a warm-up, module activity, or pre-writing task.

Canvas-ready direction:
Complete the assigned Literary Analysis Lab. Retry choices as needed. When you finish, use the Reflect button and answer the activity-specific reflection in Canvas.

4. Use Triage Before Polishing

Best once students have a substantial draft.

Canvas-ready direction:
Run your draft through Literary Analysis Triage before proofreading. Start with the first high-impact issue it identifies, complete the suggested lab if useful, and revise that part of your draft before moving to sentence-level edits.

Reflection & Completion

  1. Students complete the interactive practice. Wrong choices receive explanatory feedback and can be retried.
  2. The completion screen can be screenshotted if you want simple proof of completion.
  3. Reflect opens an activity-specific prompt.
  4. The prompt tells students to write in Canvas, not on the Literary Analysis Labs site.

Recommendation: If reflection is the learning evidence you care about, grade the Canvas reflection rather than the screenshot. The screenshot is useful mainly as lightweight completion evidence.

Using the Labs in Canvas

Direct links are usually simplest, especially on phones. You can paste any student link into a Canvas module, page, assignment, announcement, or discussion.

If you prefer an embed, expand “Canvas embed code” under a lab above and paste the iframe into the Canvas HTML editor. A height of 900 pixels is provided as a practical starting point.

If Canvas or a mobile browser makes an embed feel cramped, use the direct public link instead.
