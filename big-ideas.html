Big Ideas & Questions for Literary Analysis

Literary analysis often explores big human questions about power, identity, belonging, freedom, memory, justice, love, truth, and change. You can use these ideas as lenses when you are trying to figure out what a text might be saying.

A big idea is not automatically a theme. “Power” is a topic. A theme is a more specific idea the text develops about power. Start with a question, return to the text, and let the evidence shape your interpretation.

How to Use These Questions

1 Notice a pattern.

What keeps repeating, changing, conflicting, or standing out?

2 Choose a useful big idea.

Pick the one that actually fits what the text is doing. Do not force every lens onto every text.

3 Ask one or two questions.

Use them to look again at the text, not as questions you must answer all at once.

4 Build a possible claim.

What is this particular text suggesting about the larger idea?

Relationships & Society

These lenses can help when a text is concerned with who has influence, who belongs, what people owe one another, or how social rules shape individual lives.

Big Idea

Power & Control

Who gets to decide, define, punish, reward, speak, or set the rules?

Look for

commands, permissions, silence, surveillance, money, status, physical space, rules, threats, rewards, who interrupts whom, who must ask.

  • Who has power here, and how do we know?
  • How is power gained, maintained, challenged, or lost?
  • Who benefits from the rules or expectations in the text?
  • When do characters obey power, resist it, or internalize it?
  • Does the text treat power as visible, hidden, necessary, corrupting, protective, or something more complicated?
From topic to interpretation

Topic: Power

Possible claim: The story suggests that power can become most effective when people begin enforcing its rules on themselves.

Big Idea

Belonging & Exclusion

Who gets to belong, and what does belonging require?

Look for

insiders and outsiders, invitations, doors, groups, names, rituals, clothing, language, family expectations, acceptance, rejection.

  • Who is included, excluded, welcomed, or watched?
  • What must a character hide, change, prove, or give up in order to belong?
  • Who gets to decide what counts as “normal” or acceptable?
  • Can belonging become a form of pressure?
  • What happens when a character belongs to more than one community?
Possible claim

The text presents belonging as comforting but conditional: the character is accepted only when she keeps part of herself invisible.

Big Idea

Freedom & Constraint

What choices are genuinely available, and what limits those choices?

Look for

doors, roads, boundaries, obligations, laws, money, fear, family expectations, physical confinement, repeated decisions.

  • What does freedom mean to different characters?
  • Which limits are external, and which have become internal?
  • What is the cost of freedom in this text?
  • Can safety and freedom come into conflict?
  • Does having a choice necessarily mean a person is free?
Big Idea

Justice, Fairness & Responsibility

What do people owe one another, and who is held accountable?

Look for

punishment, blame, apology, forgiveness, unequal consequences, rules, institutions, revenge, protection, sacrifice.

  • Who decides what is fair?
  • Are characters judged by the same standards?
  • What is the difference between justice and revenge here?
  • Who is expected to take responsibility, and who escapes it?
  • Does the text suggest that following the rules is always the same as doing what is right?
Big Idea

Family, Love & Obligation

When do care, loyalty, duty, and control begin to overlap?

Look for

caregiving, sacrifice, secrecy, expectations, inheritance, repeated family roles, protection, guilt, affection, resentment.

  • What does love require in this text?
  • When does loyalty become pressure or obligation?
  • Who gives care, and whose care is taken for granted?
  • What does a character owe family, and who decided that?
  • Can love and resentment exist at the same time?
Big Idea

Class, Work & Material Conditions

How do money, labor, property, or economic position shape what characters can do?

Look for

jobs, housing, food, clothing, debt, ownership, leisure, dependence, access, status, who serves and who is served.

  • How does money expand or limit a character’s choices?
  • Whose work is visible, invisible, valued, or ignored?
  • How do characters understand success or security?
  • What assumptions do characters make about people with more or less money?
  • How does material need affect relationships, morality, or freedom?

Self & Identity

These questions help when a text explores who someone is, who they are expected to be, or how identity changes across situations.

Big Idea

Identity & Selfhood

How does a person understand who they are, and what pressures shape that understanding?

Look for

names, mirrors, clothing, roles, labels, secrets, performances, changes in first-person language, conflict between public and private selves.

  • Who does the character believe they are?
  • Who do other people expect them to be?
  • When does the character perform a version of themselves for others?
  • Which parts of identity feel chosen, assigned, inherited, or resisted?
  • What changes when the character sees themselves differently?
Big Idea

Desire, Fear & Choice

What do characters want, what are they afraid of, and what happens when those forces conflict?

Look for

hesitation, repeated choices, avoidance, contradictions, dreams, warnings, impulses, things a character reaches toward or backs away from.

  • What does the character say they want? What might they want underneath that?
  • What fear shapes their choices?
  • What do they repeatedly avoid?
  • When do desire and responsibility conflict?
  • What does the character’s choice reveal that their words do not?
Big Idea

Place, Home & Displacement

What makes a place feel like home, and what happens when that relationship changes?

Look for

houses, rooms, borders, landscapes, journeys, repeated places, objects carried from place to place, arrival and departure.

  • What makes a place feel safe, familiar, threatening, or unreachable?
  • Can someone feel at home in more than one place?
  • What changes when a character leaves or returns?
  • How are place and identity connected?
  • Can home be both comforting and restrictive?

Knowledge, Voice & Perception

These lenses are useful when a text asks who knows, who speaks, whose version is believed, or how language shapes reality.

Big Idea

Truth, Knowledge & Perception

What can be known, and how reliable is anyone’s understanding?

Look for

secrets, rumors, conflicting versions, unreliable narration, limited point of view, mistaken assumptions, withheld information.

  • Who knows what, and when do they know it?
  • What does the reader know that a character does not?
  • How does limited perspective shape the story?
  • What happens when characters mistake belief for fact?
  • Does the text suggest that truth is discoverable, partial, contested, or dependent on perspective?
Big Idea

Voice, Silence & Language

Who gets to speak, who is heard, and what happens when something cannot or will not be said?

Look for

interruptions, silence, repetition, euphemism, indirect language, naming, withheld speech, letters, stories within stories, who narrates.

  • Who gets to tell the story?
  • Whose voice is absent or filtered through someone else?
  • What cannot be said directly?
  • When does silence protect someone, and when does it harm them?
  • How does naming something change its power?
Possible claim

The play treats silence as both protection and surrender: refusing to speak keeps the character safe in the moment but allows another person’s version of events to become the accepted truth.

Big Idea

Appearance, Performance & Reality

What is shown to others, and what remains underneath?

Look for

masks, clothing, social roles, public/private spaces, lying, acting, politeness, reputation, mirrors, staged behavior.

  • What does a character want others to believe?
  • What gap exists between appearance and reality?
  • When is performance necessary for survival or belonging?
  • Who sees through the performance, and who does not?
  • Does the text suggest there is a “true self” underneath social roles?

Time, Change & Meaning

These questions help when the text is shaped by memory, loss, growth, repetition, change, or the difficulty of moving forward.

Big Idea

Memory, Time & the Past

How does the past continue to shape the present?

Look for

flashbacks, repeated memories, old objects, photographs, inherited stories, anniversaries, returns, gaps in memory, changes in tense.

  • What does the character remember, forget, revise, or refuse to remember?
  • Why does a memory appear at this particular moment?
  • How does the past shape present choices?
  • Is memory presented as reliable, selective, healing, painful, or creative?
  • What can and cannot be left behind?
Big Idea

Change, Growth & Resistance

What changes, what refuses to change, and what does change cost?

Look for

before/after contrasts, repeated mistakes, turning points, refusals, new language, changed relationships, endings that echo beginnings.

  • What actually changes by the end?
  • What stays stubbornly the same?
  • Is change chosen, forced, resisted, or incomplete?
  • What does growth require a character to give up?
  • Does the text challenge the idea that change is always progress?
Big Idea

Loss, Mortality & Meaning

How do people respond to absence, endings, vulnerability, or the fact that things do not last?

Look for

empty spaces, objects left behind, illness, death, seasons, silence, rituals, memorials, repetition, attempts to preserve something.

  • How does loss change the meaning of ordinary objects or places?
  • What does a character try to preserve?
  • How do people make meaning from something they cannot change?
  • What forms of grief are visible or invisible?
  • Does the text imagine acceptance as letting go, remembering, continuing, or something else?
Big Idea

Humanity, Nature & the Larger World

How does the text position human beings in relation to nature, place, technology, or forces larger than themselves?

Look for

weather, landscapes, animals, machines, cities, environmental change, seasons, scale, boundaries between human and nonhuman.

  • Is nature presented as resource, threat, refuge, witness, or something else?
  • What assumptions does the text make about human control?
  • How does technology change relationships or perception?
  • Where does the text blur the boundary between human and nonhuman?
  • What happens when characters confront forces they cannot control?

Do Not Force a Big Idea Onto the Text

These are possible lenses, not a checklist. A strong interpretation starts with what the text actually gives you. If you have to ignore important details to make a lens fit, try a different question.

You also do not need to decide that a text is simply “for” or “against” something. Literary texts often explore tensions: freedom can feel frightening, love can include resentment, belonging can require sacrifice, and power can protect as well as control.

Try It With Your Text

  1. Write down one pattern, tension, repeated image, choice, or conflict you have noticed.
  2. Choose one big idea on this page that seems connected to that pattern.
  3. Pick one question that genuinely makes you curious.
  4. Return to the text and find two or three details that help you think about the question.
  5. Try a sentence that begins: “The text may suggest that…”

You are not trying to discover the one hidden message. You are building an interpretation and checking whether the text supports it.
